Space exploration is the use of astronomy and space technology to explore outer space. While the exploration of space is carried out mainly by astronomers with telescopes, its physical exploration is conducted both by uncrewed robotic space probes and human spaceflight. Space exploration, like its classical form of land exploration, is one of the primary sources for space science.

The Space Age began with the launch of Sputnik 1 by the Soviet Union in October 1957. This historical milestone was followed by the American Apollo program, which achieved the first human Moon landing in July 1969 with Apollo 11. Astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the lunar surface, collecting samples and deploying scientific experiments while Michael Collins orbited above.

In recent decades, robotic exploration of the Solar System has advanced dramatically. Space probes like Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 have traversed the outer reaches of the planetary system and entered interstellar space. Planetary rovers, including Curiosity and Perseverance, explore the Martian surface searching for signs of ancient biosignatures, analyzing soil compositions, and testing oxygen production technologies.

Telescopes in space, such as the Hubble Space Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope, provide unprecedented observations of the universe. By escaping atmospheric distortion, these instruments observe distant galaxies formed shortly after the Big Bang, examine the chemical atmospheric compositions of exoplanets, and investigate dark matter and dark energy.

Commercial spaceflight has emerged as a major force in modern exploration. Private aerospace companies develop reusable rockets to dramatically lower the cost of orbital payload delivery. Plans for permanent lunar research bases under the Artemis program and prospective human missions to Mars represent the next major horizon in space exploration.
Human physiology undergoes substantial adaptation during long-duration orbital missions aboard the International Space Station. Microgravity environments induce progressive skeletal muscle atrophy and bone mineral density loss, requiring astronauts to engage in daily high-intensity resistive exercise. Fluid redistributions toward the upper body cause cephalad fluid shifts, neuro-ocular syndrome, and alterations in cardiovascular stroke volume. Extended missions beyond the Earth magnetosphere expose crews to dangerous galactic cosmic rays and solar particle events, prompting intensive aerospace biomedical research into advanced shielding materials, active electromagnetic deflector fields, and pharmaceutical radioprotectants.

Rocket propulsion systems represent the energetic foundation of space launch architecture. Chemical rocket engines rely on the combustion of cryogenic liquid propellants, such as liquid hydrogen or liquid methane paired with liquid oxygen, to produce high thrust-to-weight ratios required for escaping Earth gravity well. For deep-space propulsion, electric propulsion technologies like Hall-effect thrusters and gridded ion drives deliver dramatically superior specific impulse by ionizing inert gases like xenon or krypton and accelerating ions through high-voltage electrostatic grids, allowing probes like Dawn and BepiColombo to travel millions of kilometers using minimal propellant mass.

Astrobiology investigates the potential existence and distribution of extraterrestrial biological activity throughout the cosmos. Within the Solar System, attention focuses on ocean worlds—icy moons harboring vast subsurface liquid oceans warmed by tidal gravitational heating. Jupiter moon Europa possesses a global water ocean beneath its fractured icy crust with hydrothermal vents, while Saturn moon Enceladus erupts cryovolcanic geysers venting organic compounds, water vapor, and silica grains directly into space. Robotic astrobiology missions to these icy satellites carry specialized spectrometer instruments to detect amino acids, lipid structures, and chiral molecular imbalances that would indicate biological origin.

The exploration of exoplanetary atmospheres represents a primary frontier of astronomical science. Ground-based observatories and space-borne infrared spectrographs detect exoplanet transits across host star faces, capturing absorption spectra imprinted on filtered starlight. By deciphering spectral transmission lines, astronomers identify atmospheric constituents including water vapor, carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, and methane. Detecting chemical disequilibrium combinations, such as simultaneous methane and oxygen, could provide compelling remote biosignature evidence for photosynthetic and metabolic activity on rocky terrestrial exoplanets orbiting within stellar habitable zones.

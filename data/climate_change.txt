Climate change refers to long-term shifts in global temperatures and weather patterns over extended periods. While climate variations have occurred naturally throughout Earth history, scientific consensus indicates that human industrial activities since the industrial revolution are the primary driver of rapid global warming observed over the past century.

The primary mechanism driving contemporary climate change is the enhanced greenhouse effect. Human combustion of fossil fuels, including coal, oil, and natural gas, releases massive quantities of carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide into the atmosphere. These heat-trapping gases absorb thermal infrared radiation emitted by the Earth surface, preventing it from radiating back into space.

The consequences of rising planetary temperatures are evident across every geographic region. Polar ice sheets and mountain glaciers are melting at accelerating rates, contributing to global sea-level rise and coastal flooding. The frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, including hurricanes, prolonged heatwaves, and severe droughts, have escalated, disrupting global agriculture and threatening freshwater supplies.

Ocean acidification represents another critical consequence of increased carbon emissions. As oceans absorb approximately thirty percent of atmospheric carbon dioxide, chemical reactions produce carbonic acid, lowering seawater pH. This acidification impairs the calcification processes of marine organisms, damaging coral reef ecosystems and threatening marine food chains.

Mitigating climate change requires global systemic decarbonization. Transitioning energy infrastructure from fossil fuels toward renewable energy sources, enhancing battery storage capabilities, improving industrial energy efficiency, and protecting tropical forests are essential strategies for limiting global temperature rise to safe thresholds.
Paleoclimatology examines ancient climate history through natural paleoclimate proxy archives, including polar ice cores, marine sediment layers, tree rings, and speleothems. Ice core records extracted from deep Antarctic and Greenland ice sheets preserve trapped ancient atmospheric gas bubbles, providing direct empirical measurements of atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations and isotopic temperature proxies spanning over eight hundred thousand years. These geological records demonstrate an intimate coupling between carbon dioxide levels and planetary temperature regimes across glacial-interglacial cycles, contextualizing current human-induced emission spikes as unprecedented in geological velocity.

The Earth climate system features critical tipping points where incremental warming triggers non-linear, irreversible structural shifts. Major climate tipping elements include the destabilization of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet, the thawing of Arctic permafrost containing vast stores of preserved organic carbon, the dieback of the Amazon tropical rainforest, and the slowing of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation. The release of potent greenhouse gases like methane from thawing circumpolar peatlands and subsea clathrates creates dangerous positive feedback loops, where warming accelerates further warming and diminishes planetary natural carbon sink capacity.

Global climate change profoundly impacts agricultural productivity, water security, and human epidemiological vulnerability. Shifting rainfall patterns, seasonal shifts, and expanding arid zones compromise staple grain yields in tropical and subtropical agricultural regions. Prolonged droughts deplete subterranean aquifers and diminish glacial meltwater inputs essential for downstream irrigation and municipal consumption. Concurrently, warmer regional temperatures expand the geographical and elevational range of disease vectors, increasing population exposure to vector-borne pathogens including malaria, dengue fever, and Lyme disease.

International climate diplomacy and governance operate under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. The landmark 2015 Paris Agreement established legally binding international commitments to limit global average temperature increase to well below two degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, with ambitious efforts aimed at restricting warming to one point five degrees Celsius. Achieving these targets requires nationally determined contributions, carbon pricing mechanisms, international clean technology transfer to developing nations, and large-scale industrial carbon capture, utilization, and storage infrastructure.

Economics is the social science concerned with the production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services. It investigates how individuals, businesses, and governments allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited human needs and desires, analyzing incentives, market interactions, and aggregate financial indicators.

Microeconomics focuses on individual decision-makers and specific market mechanisms. It examines how the principles of supply and demand determine equilibrium prices in competitive markets. Consumer theory studies utility maximization under budget constraints, while producer theory evaluates production functions, marginal costs, and optimal output levels to maximize profits.

Macroeconomics examines broader economic systems at the regional, national, and global levels. Key macroeconomic indicators include gross domestic product, which measures total economic output, unemployment rates, and inflation rates. Economists evaluate business cycles, analyzing the underlying causes of economic recessions and the dynamics of long-term sustainable growth.

Monetary and fiscal policies serve as primary levers for governmental economic stabilization. Central banks implement monetary policy by adjusting benchmark interest rates and regulating bank reserves to manage liquidity and stabilize prices. Governments deploy fiscal policy through taxation adjustments and public spending infrastructure initiatives to stimulate economic aggregate demand.

Financial markets facilitate capital formation and risk management across international economies. Equity markets allow corporations to raise investment capital by issuing shares, while bond markets permit governments and corporations to borrow funds. Derivative instruments, such as options and futures, enable market participants to hedge against fluctuations in commodity prices, currencies, and interest rates.
Behavioral economics bridges economic theory with psychological insights to study why human economic actors frequently deviate from neoclassical assumptions of perfect rationality, complete information, and utility maximization. Pioneered by Daniel Kahneman, Amos Tversky, and Richard Thaler, prospect theory reveals that individuals perceive losses and gains asymmetrically, displaying profound loss aversion where the pain of losing capital exceeds the pleasure of gaining an equivalent amount. Mental accounting, hyperbolic discounting, and herd mentality in speculative financial asset bubbles demonstrate how emotional and cognitive limitations govern real-world market outcomes.

International trade theory examines the macroeconomic patterns, commercial welfare gains, and distributional consequences of cross-border commerce among sovereign states. David Ricardo model of comparative advantage demonstrates that two countries can mutually benefit from bilateral trade by specializing in goods they produce at lower relative opportunity costs, even if one country maintains an absolute productivity advantage in all commodities. Modern trade policy navigates tensions between multilateral free trade agreements and protectionist trade tariffs, import quotas, and strategic domestic industrial subsidies aimed at shielding domestic employment and critical supply chain sovereignty.

Central banking systems and monetary transmission mechanisms are vital for macroeconomic stability. Central banks, such as the Federal Reserve, European Central Bank, and Bank of Japan, manage the dual mandate of price stability and maximum sustainable employment. Beyond conventional open-market operations and benchmark interest rate adjustments, modern central banks deploy unconventional monetary tools during financial crises, including quantitative easing asset purchases, forward guidance communications, and emergency standing liquidity facilities to restore commercial credit intermediation and lower long-term corporate borrowing costs.

Corporate valuation, capital structure, and asset pricing models formulate the core mathematical framework of corporate finance. The Modigliani-Miller theorem outlines baseline conditions under which a firm debt-equity capital structure does not influence its intrinsic market value in frictionless financial markets. The Capital Asset Pricing Model mathematically prices financial securities by calculating expected returns as a linear function of risk-free interest rates and systematic market covariance risk, represented by beta, establishing foundational principles for modern portfolio construction and risk-adjusted capital budgeting.

Aviation and aerodynamics encompass the scientific principles and engineering technologies that enable heavier-than-air vehicles to achieve sustained, controlled flight through atmospheric air masses. Aerodynamics investigates the fluid dynamics of moving air interacting with solid bodies, analyzing force equilibria across four fundamental vectors: aerodynamic lift, gravitational weight, propulsive engine thrust, and viscous parasitic drag.

Aerodynamic lift is generated when an engineered cambered airfoil slices through an oncoming airstream, establishing a circulation field and asymmetric pressure distribution across upper and lower wing surfaces. According to Bernoulli principles and conservation of momentum, air flowing over the curved upper surface experiences accelerated velocity and decreased static pressure relative to the higher static pressure under the lower wing, producing an upward net pressure force perpendicular to relative wind direction.

Total aircraft drag consists of parasitic drag and lift-induced drag. Parasitic drag encompasses skin friction drag caused by fluid shear within the viscous boundary layer alongside form drag generated by aerodynamic pressure separation behind blunt airframe components. Lift-induced drag, conversely, arises as an unavoidable byproduct of lift generation, resulting from high-pressure air curling around wingtips into trailing vortex cores that deflect local airflow downward into downwash angles.

Aircraft propulsion relies predominantly on gas turbine turbofan engines to generate forward thrust through Newton third law of action and reaction. Modern high-bypass turbofans ingest immense volumes of atmospheric air through large composite titanium front fan blades, routing the vast majority of airflow around the central engine core rather than through the combustion chamber. This high bypass ratio dramatically improves fuel efficiency and reduces engine acoustic emissions during cruise flight altitudes.

Supersonic aerodynamics introduces compressible fluid phenomena when aircraft velocities approach and exceed the local speed of sound, characterized by Mach number ratios greater than unity. As air accelerates past acoustic velocity limits, it cannot compress smoothly, condensing instead into discontinuous shock waves that cause sudden pressure spikes and steep wave drag increments across leading wing edges and airframe fuselages.

Aerodynamic stalls occur when an airfoil exceeds its critical angle of attack, typically around fifteen to eighteen degrees depending on wing profile geometry. Beyond this critical boundary angle, adverse pressure gradients force the boundary airflow to detach violently from the upper wing surface, creating turbulent chaotic vortex shedding that causes sudden catastrophic loss of lift accompanied by a sharp escalation in aerodynamic form drag.
